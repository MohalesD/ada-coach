### Q03 — Tier 1 (High retrieval relevance)

**Question:** What makes a good customer discovery question versus a bad one?

**Retrieved chunks:** none above threshold

**Arm A (RAG on):**

A good question gives you *honest signal about past behavior*. A bad one gets you polite agreement or future intentions that don't predict reality.

Here's the hard distinction:

**Good questions isolate one thing at a time.** "Walk me through the last time you tried to solve this problem" is better than "How do you usually handle X?" The first anchors to a real moment; the second invites a sanitized summary.

**Good questions don't mention your solution.** The moment you say "Would you use a tool that does X?", you've stopped listening to their world and started testing your idea. They'll say yes because you've framed the answer for them.

**Good questions distinguish between symptom and root cause.** "How often does this slow you down?" (symptom) is weaker than "What happens as a result? Who else feels it? What have you already tried?" (cause and consequence). Symptoms are everywhere. Root causes are rare.

**Good questions make lying hard.** If someone tells you they spend 8 hours a week on a task but then can't name a single workaround they've tried, you've found your signal—the problem might not be as expensive as they claimed.

The test: Could the person you're interviewing answer the same question the exact same way if your product didn't exist? If yes, it's a discovery question. If no, you're selling.

What's driving your question—are you working through a specific discovery challenge, or building your skill here?

**Arm B (RAG off):**

A good discovery question reveals behavior; a bad one invites polite fiction.

Good questions ask *what someone actually did* in the past: "Walk me through the last time you faced this problem. What did you try first?" Bad questions ask what they *would* do or *might* do: "Would you use a tool that did X?" The second one gets you "sure, sounds useful"—which tells you almost nothing.

Good questions narrow in on specific friction: "How much time did that manual process take?" Bad ones stay vague: "Is this hard for you?" Good questions shut down storytelling and push for evidence: "Show me how you do it today. What tools are you using right now?" Bad ones let people perform the version of themselves they think you want to hear.

The sharpest distinction: good questions make people slightly uncomfortable because they're concrete and specific. Bad questions feel easy to answer because they're abstract or flattering.

The constraint that separates them is the **Mom Test principle**—ask questions you'd ask your mom (someone with no stake in your success), about her *actual past behavior*, never about your solution. If your question only makes sense because your product exists, it's not a discovery question. It's a sales question wearing a disguise.

### Q11 — Tier 2 (Medium retrieval relevance)

**Question:** How do I avoid leading questions in user interviews?

**Retrieved chunks:** none above threshold

**Arm A (RAG on):**

Leading questions smuggle your answer into the question itself—they make it easy for users to agree with you rather than tell you what's actually true. The Mom Test calls this "investor heroin": it *feels* like validation, but it's just politeness.

Three practical moves:

**Remove the solution hint.** Instead of "Do you struggle with manually entering data into spreadsheets?" ask "Walk me through how you currently track that information." You get their actual workflow, not their agreement with your framing.

**Ask about past behavior, not future intentions.** "Would you use a tool that did X?" invites a polite yes. "How have you solved this in the past?" gets you what they actually do when they have a real problem.

**Listen for the story, not the yes.** If someone says "Yeah, that's annoying," that's noise. If they say "Last week I spent three hours fixing duplicate entries because our system doesn't talk to our CRM," that's signal. The second one proves the problem exists; the first just proves they're being nice.

The sharpest question is often the simplest one: "Tell me about the last time you ran into this."

**Arm B (RAG off):**

Leading questions telegraph the answer you want to hear, which kills honest signal. The antidote is simple: ask about what they've *already done*, not what they might do.

Here's the shift:

**Leading:** "Would you use a tool that automated your weekly reporting?"
**Open:** "Walk me through how you handled your last reporting cycle. What took the most time?"

The first plants your solution in their head. The second lets them show you the real problem without your bias in the room.

Three tactics:

1. **Flip to past behavior.** Never ask "Would you...?" Ask "How did you...?" or "Tell me about the last time..." Past behavior is honest. Future intentions are polite fiction.

2. **Remove your solution from the question.** Don't mention automation, dashboards, AI, or your product idea. Let them describe their world first. You'll see the gaps yourself.

3. **Ask about the cost, not the desire.** Instead of "Do you wish reporting was faster?" ask "How much time does reporting actually take each week?" Let the number speak for itself.

The Mom Test covers this brilliantly—it's worth reading the chapter on asking honest questions. But the core rule is: you're investigating their life, not validating your idea.
